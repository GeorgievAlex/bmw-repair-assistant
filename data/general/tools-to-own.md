# Tools worth owning for these jobs

Working out what you actually need before starting a job, rather than discovering it with the car on stands and the parts shop closed. Grouped by how often you'll reach for them across the procedures on this site.

## You will use these constantly

A socket set with extensions and a universal joint. Half the difficulty on this chassis is access, not the fastener itself, and an extension plus a swivel turns several "impossible" bolts into ordinary ones.

A torque wrench, ideally two: a smaller one covering roughly 5 to 60 Nm for valve covers, oil filter caps and sensors, and a larger one up to around 200 Nm for wheel bolts and suspension work. One wrench spanning that whole range accurately doesn't really exist at a sensible price.

Jack and axle stands. Never work under a car held up by a jack alone, this is the one piece of advice in here that is about whether you get up again.

Penetrating fluid. On a car this age, in any climate with winter salt, this is not optional and it works best applied well before you need the bolt to move, not while you're swearing at it.

## Needed for specific jobs here

An angle gauge, or a torque wrench that does angle tightening. Several fasteners on this chassis are torque-to-yield, specified as a torque figure plus a further rotation in degrees, and guessing the angle defeats the point of the spec.

A ball joint separator, for the control arm and tie rod procedures. Hammering a knuckle instead is how people damage a part they weren't planning to replace.

An oil filter housing wrench, typically 36mm for this engine.

A caliper piston compression tool for brake work, plus the correct retraction method if your rear calipers are the electric parking brake type, where forcing the piston damages the motor.

## Specialist, worth renting rather than buying

An RTAB removal and installation tool kit, for the rear trailing arm bushings. Many parts stores loan these. Improvising damages the trailing arm.

A spring compressor, only if you're reusing springs on the front struts. Read the warning in that procedure first: buying complete pre-assembled strut units costs more and removes the hazard entirely, which for an occasional job is usually the better trade.

A cooling system pressure tester, which finds the cracked-pipe leaks that leave no puddle far faster than staring at the ground.

## Diagnostic

A basic OBD2 reader will pull generic engine codes and is better than nothing. BMW-specific software (INPA, ISTA and similar, widely discussed in owner communities) reads the body, chassis and transmission modules that generic readers can't see, and is what you need for things like registering a new battery.

## What you don't need

A full workshop. Every procedure on this site is written assuming a driveway, hand tools, and patience, with the specialist items above called out individually in the job that needs them. If a procedure needs something unusual, it says so in its Tools row.
