# Before you start any job

The habits that separate a job that takes an afternoon from one that takes a weekend and a tow truck. None of this is specific to one procedure, which is why it isn't repeated on every page.

## Buy the parts before you touch the car

Obvious, routinely ignored. A car on stands with the wheel off is not a car you can drive to the parts shop. Check the part actually matches before you start disassembly too, a wrong-variant part discovered at the halfway point is the single most common reason a Saturday job becomes a Monday problem.

## Photograph everything as you go

Before disconnecting a connector, routing a belt, or removing a bracket, photograph it. You will not remember which of the three similar clips went where. This costs seconds and saves genuinely painful reassembly guesswork.

## Keep fasteners organised by where they came from

Bolts on this chassis are frequently different lengths in the same bracket. An egg box, a magnetic tray with sections, or just labelled bags, anything that preserves which bolt came from which hole.

## Check whether your fasteners are single-use

Several procedures here involve torque-to-yield bolts, which stretch permanently when tightened and must not be reused. Alternator mounting bolts, some suspension fasteners, and some valve cover designs. Find out before you start, not when you're ready to reassemble.

## Work on a cold car

Applies to anything touching the cooling system or exhaust. A pressurised cooling system at operating temperature will spray scalding coolant when opened. "Warm enough that oil flows" and "hot" are different states, and only the oil change wants the former.

## Disconnect the battery for electrical work

Negative terminal first. Bear in mind on this generation that a battery disconnect can require re-learning window positions afterward, and that a replacement battery needs registering, see the battery procedure.

## Know your stopping point

Decide in advance what you'll do if a bolt rounds off, a part arrives wrong, or something behind the part you're replacing turns out to be worse. Having a plan for "this isn't going to finish today" before you start is what keeps the car driveable or at least safely parked rather than stuck mid-teardown.

## Torque bushings at ride height

Any fastener passing through a rubber suspension bushing should get its final torque with the suspension loaded at normal ride height, not hanging on a jack. Torqued at full droop, the bushing sits permanently twisted and tears early. This catches people on control arms, trailing arm bushings and rear shocks alike.

## Get an alignment after suspension or steering work

Not optional, not a nice-to-have. Anything that moves control arms, struts, or tie rods changes the geometry, and driving on it unaligned eats tyres and affects how the car behaves under braking.

## When to stop and get help

Brakes that don't feel right, steering with play you can't account for, or a temperature gauge climbing while driving are not things to work through by elimination. Neither is anything you're not confident about with the car's weight above you. There's no shame in a job going to a shop, and considerably less shame than the alternative.
